## Shopping List: Large-Print Talking Bathroom Scale for Low Vision

- **Large-print, high-contrast display** (e.g., 2-inch+ digits, black-on-white or white-on-black)
- **Clear, adjustable-volume voice announcement** (with optional mute function)
- **Capacity of at least 400 lbs (180 kg)** with 0.2 lb or 0.1 kg increments
- **Step-on activation** (no bending to tap buttons) or large, tactile power button
- **Non-slip platform** and wide, stable base
- **Measurement units switchable** (pounds, kilograms, stones)
- **Auto-shutoff** to conserve battery
- **Battery type** (AAA or lithium) with low-battery indicator
- **Memory function** (optional but helpful for tracking trends)

## Buying Guide

### Why Large-Print, High-Contrast Display Matters
For an elderly woman with low vision, the primary obstacle is reading the numbers. A standard scale’s small, low-contrast digits (often gray on silver) are nearly invisible. Look for scales with **2-inch or larger digits** in a high-contrast color scheme—**pure black on a bright white background** (or white on black). Avoid scales with glossy reflective surfaces, which create glare and wash out the numbers. The best models use **backlit LCD or LED displays** with adjustable brightness, ensuring readability in dim bathroom lighting.

### Voice Announcement: Volume and Clarity Are Non-Negotiable
The “talking” feature should not be an afterthought. Prioritize scales with a **clear, synthesized voice** that is not muffled by the speaker’s enclosure. Test (via reviews) that the volume can be **adjusted up to at least 75 dB**—enough to overcome ambient noise like running water or a television. Crucially, the scale should have a **mute toggle** for times when silence is preferred (e.g., weighing in a shared space). Some models only speak at a fixed volume; avoid those if hearing loss is a factor. Also confirm the voice speaks in the user’s native language (typically English or Spanish).

### Capacity and Precision: Safety and Consistent Data
A scale that maxes out too low creates a safety hazard (the user might lean or step off abruptly). Choose a **minimum capacity of 400 lbs (180 kg)** to accommodate fluctuations in weight, water retention, or clothing. Precision matters too: **0.2 lb or 0.1 kg increments** are ideal for tracking small changes, which is helpful for managing health conditions like edema or diabetes. Avoid scales that only display whole numbers (e.g., 150 lbs vs. 150.4 lbs)—they hide gradual trends.

### Step-On Activation vs. Manual Buttons
Bending to tap a small button is a fall risk and physically taxing. Select a scale with **step-on activation** (auto-on when you stand on it). The scale should then wait a few seconds, read your weight, and announce it without needing to press anything. If the scale requires a button, ensure that button is **large, tactile, and brightly colored** (e.g., bright yellow or orange) and located on the top surface, not the side.

### Stability and Safety: Non-Slip and Wide Base
Elderly users often have balance issues. The scale must have a **wide, stable base** (at least 12 inches wide) and a **non-slip rubberized platform** that grips bare feet. Avoid narrow, plastic “glass platform” scales that can shift or slide on tile. The scale’s underside should have **four large rubber feet** that prevent it from skidding. If the user uses a walker, consider a scale with a **low-profile platform** (under 1.5 inches tall) to minimize step height.

### Measurement Units: One-Click Switch
Many elderly users grew up with pounds or stones, while doctors may request kilograms. Ensure the scale includes a **simple switch (often a button on the underside or a toggle)** to change between **pounds (lb), kilograms (kg), and stones (st)** . Avoid scales that require a long press of a sequence of buttons—this is confusing for low-vision users.

### Battery Life and Indication
A dead battery in the middle of weighing is frustrating. Look for scales that use **standard AAA batteries** (easy to replace) or **long-lasting lithium coin cells** (CR2032). The scale must have a **clear, audible low-battery warning** (e.g., “Battery low” spoken aloud, not just a tiny icon on the display). Auto-shutoff is essential to prevent accidental drain.

### Memory Feature: Helpful, Not Confusing
Some talking scales store the last 5–10 weigh-ins. For a user with low vision, this can be useful for identifying trends without writing down numbers. However, the memory function should be **simple to access**—ideally by pressing a single labeled button (e.g., “Memory”) that announces the previous weights in order. Avoid models that require navigating multiple menus or using a smartphone app, which adds unnecessary complexity.

### Additional Consideration: Scale Placement
Even the best scale won’t help if it’s placed on a soft rug or carpet. The scale must sit on a **hard, flat surface** (tile, linoleum, hardwood). If the bathroom has carpet, consider buying a **low-profile, hard plastic platform** to place under the scale for stability.
