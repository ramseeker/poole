## Bulleted Shopping List of Essential Items

- **Tulip bulbs** (species tulips recommended for naturalizing, or Darwin Hybrid for longevity)
- **Daffodil bulbs** (large-cupped or trumpet varieties for first-year impact)
- **Garden spade** or **trowel** (stainless steel, with depth markings)
- **Bulb planter** (manual auger or plunger-style, with depth gauge)
- **Composted organic matter** (well-rotted manure or leaf mold)
- **Bone meal** (slow-release phosphorus source, not high-nitrogen fertilizer)
- **Grit or horticultural sand** (for drainage improvement, especially in clay soil)
- **Dibber** (wooden or metal, for precise hole spacing)
- **Garden hose** with spray nozzle (or watering can)
- **Measuring tape** or **ruler** (at least 12 inches long)
- **Garden marker stakes** (with permanent marker or pencil)

## Detailed Buying Guide

### **Tulip Bulbs**

**Why this item?**  
Tulips are the quintessential spring color bomb, but not all bulbs are created equal. For a first-time homeowner, choose **Darwin Hybrid tulips** (e.g., 'Apeldoorn', 'Ad Rem') because they are perennializing, meaning they will return for 3–5 years reliably. Avoid “forced” or greenhouse-grown bulbs sold in discount bins—they often lack the vigor to survive a real winter. Species tulips (botanical tulips) like *Tulipa tarda* or *Tulipa bakeri* are smaller but more resilient, ideal for naturalizing in lawns or rock gardens.

**What to look for:**  
- **Firm, heavy bulbs** (avoid soft, moldy, or shriveled specimens).  
- **Size**: Larger bulbs (12+ cm circumference) produce bigger flowers; smaller (8–10 cm) are cheaper but better for mass planting.  
- **Disease resistance**: Look for “virus-free” labels, especially if planting near other bulbs.

---

### **Daffodil Bulbs**

**Why this item?**  
Daffodils are the most reliable perennial bulbs—they are deer-proof, rodent-resistant, and thrive in partial shade. For a first spring, choose **large-cupped** (e.g., 'Ice Follies', 'Carlton') or **trumpet daffodils** (e.g., 'King Alfred', 'Dutch Master') for instant impact. Miniature daffodils (like *Narcissus ‘Tête-à-Tête’*) are excellent for borders or pots but need deeper planting relative to their size.

**What to look for:**  
- **Double noses** (two growing points) will produce more stems per bulb.  
- **Bulb firmness**: They should be rock-hard, not spongy.  
- **Storage**: Buy from reputable suppliers who store bulbs at 60–65°F (15–18°C) to prevent pre-sprouting.

---

### **Garden Spade or Trowel with Depth Markings**

**Why this item?**  
Bulb planting depth is non-negotiable: too shallow and bulbs freeze; too deep and they rot or fail to flower. A **spade with a straight blade** allows you to cut a clean trench for mass planting, while a **trowel with inch/centimeter markings** ensures individual holes are exact. Look for stainless steel—it resists rust and cuts through clay soil better than cheap carbon steel.

**What to look for:**  
- **Ergonomic handle** (rubberized or D-grip) to avoid blisters when planting dozens of bulbs.  
- **Depth gauge** etched into the metal (e.g., marks every 1 inch/2.5 cm).  
- **Width**: A trowel with a 3–4 inch blade works for most bulbs; a spade should be 6–8 inches wide for trenches.

---

### **Bulb Planter (Auger or Plunger-Style)**

**Why this item?**  
A bulb planter saves time and frustration, especially for double-digging in heavy ground. **Manual augers** (drill-powered) are ideal for large areas; they pull out a clean core of soil, making depth consistent. **Plunger-style planters** (with a T-handle) are best for small gardens—they create a hole, then eject the bulb. Both types have depth stops that prevent over-digging.

**What to look for:**  
- **Metal core** (plastic augers snap in cold soil).  
- **Adjustable depth stop** (tulips: 6–8 inches deep; daffodils: 4–6 inches deep).  
- **Weight**: A 2lb auger is manageable; heavier models tire out wrists.

---

### **Composted Organic Matter**

**Why this item?**  
Most garden soil (especially in new subdivisions) is compacted, low in organic matter, or overly sandy. Mixing in **composted manure** or **leaf mold** improves drainage, aerates roots, and adds slow-release nutrients. Avoid fresh manure—it burns bulbs.

**What to look for:**  
- **Dark, crumbly texture** (no large sticks or visible straw).  
- **Smell**: Should be earthy, not ammonia-like.  
- **Bagged or bulk**: Bagged is convenient for small yards; bulk is cheaper for large beds. Aim for 1–2 inches worked into the top 8 inches of soil.

---

### **Bone Meal (Slow-Release Phosphorus)**

**Why this item?**  
Bulbs need **phosphorus (P)** for strong root growth and flower production, and **potassium (K)** for winter hardiness. Bone meal provides a steady phosphorus release over 4–6 months. *Crucially, do not use high-nitrogen fertilizers* (like granular lawn food)—they encourage soft leaf growth at the expense of blooms and can attract bulb-eating rodents.

**What to look for:**  
- **Steamed bone meal** (pastes less and doesn’t smell rancid).  
- **NPK ratio**: Look for 3-15-0 or similar (low N, high P).  
- **Application**: 1 tablespoon per bulb, worked into the soil at the hole bottom (never directly touching the bulb).

---

### **Grit or Horticultural Sand**

**Why this item?**  
If your soil is clay-heavy (sticks to your shovel, forms clogs), bulbs can rot in wet winters. **Horticultural sand** or **grit** (sharp, not beach sand) creates drainage channels around the bulb base. Mix it 1:1 with compost when backfilling holes.

**What to look for:**  
- **Particle size**: 1–3 mm (similar to coarse bird grit).  
- **Clean**: Avoid plaster sand or play sand (too fine, compacts).  
- **Volume**: A 20lb bag covers about 50 bulbs in heavy soil.

---

### **Dibber**

**Why this item?**  
A **dibber** (a pointed stick or metal tool) is perfect for creating evenly spaced, depth-calibrated holes in soft soil. It prevents wobbling and ensures consistent depth without a measuring tape each time. Use it to mark holes 4–6 inches apart for tulips and 6–8 inches for daffodils.

**What to look for:**  
- **Length**: At least 10–12 inches to reach full depth.  
- **Markings**: Some dibbers have painted bands at 4, 6, and 8 inches.  
- **Material**: Ash wood or aluminum (both resist rot).

---

### **Garden Hose with Spray Nozzle (or Watering Can)**

**Why this item?**  
After planting, **deep watering** settles the soil and eliminates air pockets, preventing bulbs from drying out or heaving during freeze-thaw cycles. A gentle spray nozzle (not a jet) avoids washing away soil. For small beds, a **watering can** with a rose head is sufficient.

**What to look for:**  
- **Adjustable spray pattern** (mist to shower).  
- **Length**: 50-foot hose for larger yards; 2-gallon can for container planting.  
- **Connections**: Brass fittings (don't rust like plastic).

---

### **Measuring Tape or Ruler**

**Why this item?**  
The **depth chart** below is worthless without precise measurement. A 12-inch ruler or flexible tape lets you check hole depth quickly (tulips: 6–8 inches; daffodils: 4–6 inches). Use it to measure from the *bottom* of the hole to the soil surface (not from the bulb tip). *Pro tip: Mark the depth on your trowel handle with a permanent marker.*

**What to look for:**  
- **Metric and imperial markings** (bulb depth guides often mix inches and centimeters).  
- **Rigid tape** (cloth tapes stretch over time).

---

### **Garden Marker Stakes**

**Why this item?**  
Bulb foliage dies back by summer, leaving you to dig into bulbs accidentally when planting annuals. Stakes prevent this. Write the bulb type, planting depth, and date. Use **indelible pencil** or **permanent marker** (rain-proof). Place stakes at the *outer edge* of the planting area, not in the center.

**What to look for:**  
- **Weatherproof material** (plastic-coated wood or UV-resistant plastic).  
- **Length**: 12–18 inches (push halfway into soil).  
- **Color**: Bright orange or yellow to be visible in spring grass.

### **First Spring Bulb Planting Depth Chart (Tulip & Daffodil)**

| Bulb Type | Soil Depth (inches) | Spacing (inches) | Notes |
|-----------|---------------------|------------------|-------|
| **Darwin Hybrid Tulip** | 6–8 (15–20 cm) | 4–6 (10–15 cm) | Plant pointed end up; deep planting deters squirrels. |
| **Species Tulip** | 4–6 (10–15 cm) | 3–4 (8–10 cm) | Smaller bulbs; can be planted shallower in thin soil. |
| **Large-Cupped Daffodil** | 4–6 (10–15 cm) | 6–8 (15–20 cm) | Plant after soil cools (September–November). |
| **Trumpet Daffodil** | 6 (15 cm) | 8 (20 cm) | Use a dibber for consistent depth; water well after planting. |
| **Miniature Daffodil** | 3–4 (8–10 cm) | 2–3 (5–8 cm) | Group in clusters for visual impact. |

**Note**: Multiply depth by 3 times the bulb’s diameter (e.g., a 2-inch bulb needs a 6-inch hole). In heavy clay, reduce depth by 1 inch to improve drainage.
