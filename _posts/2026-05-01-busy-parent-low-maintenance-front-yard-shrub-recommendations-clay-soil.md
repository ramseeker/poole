## Detailed Buying Guide

### **Arborvitae (Thuja occidentalis 'Emerald Green')**
**Why it works:** Clay soil holds moisture, and Arborvitae tolerates heavy, wet feet better than many evergreens. Its narrow, columnar shape (8-12 ft tall, 3-4 ft wide) provides year-round structure without outgrowing a small front yard.  
**Shopping logic:** Choose 3-5 gallon pots for immediate impact—these establish in clay in one season. Avoid bare-root or small 1-gallon sizes, which struggle in compacted clay. Inspect for brown tips or root-bound pots; healthy plants have vibrant green scales and firm, white roots visible at drain holes.  
**Install tip:** Dig a hole twice as wide as the pot but no deeper. Mound the root ball slightly above grade to prevent rot in heavy clay.

### **Oakleaf Hydrangea (Hydrangea quercifolia)**
**Why it works:** Native to the Southeastern U.S., this shrub thrives in clay’s nutrient-rich but heavy texture. Its lobed leaves and peeling bark add winter interest, and it blooms on old wood—so no fussy pruning.  
**Shopping logic:** Buy 2-3 gallon pots for best root-to-canopy balance in clay. Avoid “reblooming” hybrids (e.g., ‘Endless Summer’) that demand perfect drainage. Look for healthy, unspotted leaves and multiple stems. White or cream flower panicles (in season) indicate strong stock.  
**Install tip:** Mix a handful of gypsum into the backfill to loosen clay around roots. Water deeply once weekly—no daily watering needed.

### **Dwarf Fothergilla (Fothergilla gardenii)**
**Why it works:** A compact, 2-3 ft shrub with fragrant spring bottlebrush flowers and stunning fall orange-red color. It tolerates clay’s alkalinity (common in disturbed urban soils) without chlorosis.  
**Shopping logic:** Choose 1-2 gallon pots—dwarf forms are slow-growing, so smaller sizes are cost-effective and transplant easier in clay. Avoid plants with yellow leaves (sign of poor drainage). Gently tug the stem; it should resist—loose roots mean root rot.  
**Install tip:** No soil amendments needed—just loosen the clay in a wide circle. Mulch heavily (3-4 inches) to keep roots cool and reduce cracking in dry clay.

### **Virginia Sweetspire (Itea virginica 'Little Henry')**
**Why it works:** This 2-3 ft native shrub is a clay-soil superstar—it thrives in both wet and dry conditions, produces arching white flower spikes in summer, and has brilliant red fall color. Minimal pruning (remove dead wood only).  
**Shopping logic:** Opt for 1-2 gallon containers; avoid liners (plugs) which dry out too quickly in clay. Look for multiple stems emerging from the base (bushy habit) and no blackened leaves (fungi sign). ‘Little Henry’ stays tidy; larger ‘Henry’s Garnet’ may need more space.  
**Install tip:** Plant in mass for a low-maintenance groundcover effect. Water only during severe drought (3+ weeks without rain).

### **Black-Eyed Susan (Rudbeckia fulgida 'Goldsturm')**
**Why it works:** Perennial but behaves like a low-maintenance shrub in clay—it spreads slowly, blooms June to frost, and self-seeds. Clay’s high phosphorus content actually boosts flower production.  
**Shopping logic:** Buy 4-inch pots or 1-gallon plugs—larger rhizomes (bare root) often rot in clay. Choose ‘Goldsturm’ (not Rudbeckia hirta biennial) for reliable multi-year returns. Check for healthy, dark green basal leaves and no powdery mildew spots.  
**Install tip:** Space 18 inches apart. Cut back to 6 inches in late winter—no staking needed. This plant is so resilient you can skip fertilizer.

### **Organic Compost**
**Why it works:** Clay soil is mineral-dense but low in organic matter. Compost improves drainage, feeds microbes, and lightens texture.  
**Shopping logic:** Buy 2-3 cubic foot bags (e.g., Dr. Earth or local municipal compost). Avoid “topsoil” blends that are just clay with sand. Open a bag—it should smell earthy, not sour or ammonia-like.  
**Install tip:** Mix 1 part compost to 2 parts excavated clay when backfilling each shrub.

### **Clay-Breaker Gypsum**
**Why it works:** Gypsum (calcium sulfate) flocculates clay particles, creating pore space for air and water. Unlike sand, it doesn’t just fill gaps—it chemically breaks up heavy texture.  
**Shopping logic:** Pelletized form (e.g., Greenway Biotech) is easiest to broadcast. Avoid “liquid gypsum” sprays—they don’t penetrate deep enough for shrubs. One 40 lb bag treats 1,000 sq ft.  
**Install tip:** Apply 1-2 tablespoons per planting hole. Water in immediately—gypsum is only active when moist.

### **Slow-Release 10-10-10 Granular Fertilizer**
**Why it works:** Clay holds nutrients well, but new shrubs need a balanced boost for root establishment. A slow-release formula prevents salt burn in clay’s low-drainage environment.  
**Shopping logic:** Choose a reputable brand (Osmocote, Espoma). Avoid high-nitrogen (e.g., 30-0-0) which can scorch roots. Look for “for all shrubs” labels.  
**Install tip:** Sprinkle 1 tablespoon per foot of shrub height around the root zone (not touching the stem) in early spring. Skip if clay is already rich.

### **Landscape Fabric**
**Why it works:** Clay soil dries into a hard crust that cracks, attracting weeds. Fabric suppresses weeds while allowing water to percolate.  
**Shopping logic:** Buy heavy-duty, non-woven fabric (e.g., Dewitt Pro 5 oz). Avoid cheap poly-woven that tears in clay’s freeze-thaw cycles.  
**Install tip:** Cut X-shaped slits for shrubs, not circles—fabric edges fray less in clay. Overlap seams 6 inches.

### **Shredded Hardwood Mulch**
**Why it works:** Shredded bark stays in place on slopes (clay soil often runs off in rain) and decomposes slowly, adding organic matter to clay over years.  
**Shopping logic:** Choose shredded (not chunky nuggets) which interlock. Cypress and cedar are long-lasting but expensive; pine fines (shredded) work well. Avoid dyed black mulch—it can leach synthetic dyes into clay.  
**Install tip:** Apply 3 inches deep after planting. Keep 2 inches away from shrub stems to prevent crown rot in wet clay.

### **Garden Spade with Sharp Edge**
**Why it works:** Digging in clay requires a sharp, sturdy blade. A flat spade (not a round shovel) cuts through roots and compacted layers cleanly.  
**Shopping logic:** Look for a tempered steel blade with a foot tread (e.g., Fiskars or Truper). Avoid fiberglass handles—wood ash can splinter in clay’s wet/dry cycles.  
**Install tip:** Sharpen the edge every two uses with a file—dull blades smoosh clay instead of cutting it.

### **Hose with Adjustable Spray Nozzle**
**Why it works:** Clay soil absorbs water slowly; a high-pressure jet can sheet away. A nozzle with “shower” and “soak” settings lets you water gently.  
**Shopping logic:** Buy a 50-ft rubber hose (kink-resistant in clay’s uneven terrain) with a metal nozzle (e.g., Dramm). Avoid plastic nozzles that crack in freezing and thawing.  
**Install tip:** Water at a low, steady trickle for 30 minutes per shrub weekly—clay absorbs best with slow, deep watering.
