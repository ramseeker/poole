## Shopping List: Essential Items for an ADHD Child's School Success

* **Chewable Fidget Pencil Toppers** (silicone, textured, replaceable)
* **Non-Glare Graph Paper Notebooks** (e.g., 1/4" or 5mm squares, off-white or pastel paper)
* **Pencil Grip Aid** (soft, ergonomic, for the pencil itself, not the topper)
* **Zippered Pencil Case** (hard shell or fabric, labeled with child’s name)
* **Dry-Erase Sleeve** (for reusable graph paper practice)
* **Sensory Fidget Strip** (adhesive, for the desk or notebook cover)

---

## Buying Guide: The Logic Behind Each Item

### ## Chewable Fidget Pencil Toppers

**Why they work:** For many ADHD children, oral motor stimulation (chewing) can significantly improve focus and reduce anxiety during seated tasks. A standard pencil topper is a small, discrete, and school-safe way to channel this need without disrupting the classroom.

**Key considerations:**
- **Material:** Choose **food-grade silicone** (not plastic) that is firm enough to chew but soft on teeth. Avoid toppers with sharp edges or small beads that could detach.
- **Texture:** Look for **ribbed, bumpy, or fin-shaped** designs. Different textures provide varied proprioceptive input. Some children prefer smooth, others need sharp ridges.
- **Replaceability:** Children with ADHD often lose or wear down chews quickly. Buy a **multi-pack** (5-10 units) with a single pencil case to store extras. Brands like *Chewigem* or *ARK Therapeutic* offer school-safe options.
- **Safety:** Ensure the topper has a **ventilation hole** (to avoid choking if swallowed) and is **BPA/phthalate-free**. Never let a child chew a standard plastic eraser-topper—it can splinter.

**Pro tip:** Attach the topper to a **shorter pencil** (e.g., Ticonderoga #2, pre-sharpened) so the weight is balanced. A long pencil + heavy topper can tip in the hand.

### ## Non-Glare Graph Paper Notebooks

**Why they work:** Children with ADHD and/or sensory processing issues (common co-occurring conditions) are often sensitive to bright white paper, which can cause visual stress, glare, and “dancing letters.” Graph paper provides a **structured visual grid** that helps with number alignment, handwriting spacing, and geometry. Non-glare paper reduces eye strain and squinting.

**Key considerations:**
- **Paper Color:** Go for **cream, off-white, or pastel** (e.g., light blue, yellow, or green). Bright white (high contrast) reflects more light and triggers sensory overload. Off-white paper is easier on the eyes.
- **Glare Reduction:** Look for **matte finish** paper, not glossy. Brands like *TOPS* or *Mead* offer “notebooks with low-glare paper.” Test by holding a page under a desk lamp—if it reflects, it’s too shiny.
- **Grid Size:** Elementary school children typically need **1/4-inch (5mm)** squares. This size allows neat number columns for math problems and room for letter practice. Avoid 1-inch squares (too large) or 1-cm squares (too small for small motor skills).
- **Binding & Durability:** Choose **spiral or wire-bound** notebooks that lay flat (easier to write without lifting pages). A **strong plastic cover** protects against spills and bending in a backpack.

**Pro tip:** Buy **one main notebook** for math, and a **second for science/drawing**. Label each notebook with a sticky note on the front: “Math – Graph Paper” so the child can quickly grab the right one.

### ## Pencil Grip Aid (Separate from Topper)

**Why you need it:** A chewy topper changes the weight and balance of the pencil. Adding a separate **ergonomic grip** (like the “The Pencil Grip” or “Eggzecutive” style) helps the child maintain a stable, tripod hold quickly. This reduces hand fatigue and frustration.

**Key features:**
- **Material:** Soft, **medical-grade silicone** that won’t slip when sweaty.
- **Positioning:** Place the grip about 1 inch above the pencil tip. The topper goes on the back end—they should not touch. This creates a “sensory sandwich” for the hand.
- **Size:** Most grips are small; ensure it fits the girth of a standard No. 2 pencil.

### ## Zippered Pencil Case

**Why it matters:** ADHD children often lose pencil ends, toppers, and grips in the bottom of a backpack. A **hard shell pencil box** (like *Lakeshore Learning* or *Crayon Box* style) or a **fabric zip pouch** with clear compartments prevents chaos.

**How to set it up:**
- **One pocket for the pencil:** (pre-sharpened, with topper and grip already attached in advance)
- **One pocket for replacement toppers** (stored in a small baggie)
- **One pocket for a spare eraser** (soft-pink, not the pencil’s end eraser)
- **Label with visible name** using a Sharpie or sticker.

### ## Dry-Erase Sleeve

**Why it’s a game-changer:** A **plastic sleeve** (any, like *Pocket Doodle* or a reusable sheet protector) allows the child to slide a piece of graph paper inside and write on the clear surface with a **dry-erase marker**. This provides:
- **Instant erasing:** No tears over mistakes—just wipe and retry. Great for math work that needs correction.
- **Repeated practice:** One sheet of graph paper lasts for weeks of dry-erase use.
- **Tactile feedback:** The smooth, cool surface can be calming for sensory-seeking children.

**Setup:** Pair with a **low-odor dry-erase marker** (like *Expo Low Odor*) and a small cloth eraser or sock. Keep in the pencil case.

### ## Adhesive Sensory Fidget Strip

**Why it’s essential:** A fidget strip (e.g., *Fidget Cube* or *Wacky Tracks* for desk, or a velcro strip) gives the child a **quiet, tactile outlet** during listening time or when the chewy topper feels too distracting. It works in tandem with the topper, not as a replacement.

**Placement:** Stick it to the **inside lid of the pencil case** or the **top edge of the notebook** (where hand rests). This allows the child to rub, fidget, or squeeze without dropping anything or looking disruptive.

**Pro tip:** Avoid strips that make noise (metallic clickers) or are translucent and easily lost. Choose a **bright color** (like neon green) so it stands out in the case.

### Final Logistics for Parents

1. **Test-drive the setup** at home for 15 minutes before sending to school. Let the child write a few math problems with the topper, grip, and non-glare paper to confirm they feel comfortable and don’t wobble.
2. **Communicate with the teacher**: Provide a brief note or email explaining that the topper is a sensory tool, not a toy. Many schools now allow “fidget pencil toppers” if they are listed as an accommodation (even without a 504 Plan, you can request permission).
3. **Budget for replacements**: Plan to buy a new pack of toppers every 2-3 months. Silicone wears down, and lanyard clips break. Keep a spare set in the child’s backpack.
4. **Don’t over-complicate**: Start with just the topper, non-glare graph paper, and the pencil case. Add the dry-erase sleeve and fidget strip only if the child shows interest. Too many items can overwhelm an ADHD brain.
