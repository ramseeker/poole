## Essential Shopping List

- **Garden Journal Template** (digital or printable: PDF, Notion, or Excel)
- **Durable Binder or Spiral Notebook** (1–2 inch ring binder or hardcover, 200+ pages)
- **Permanent Markers** (fine-tip, waterproof, black and assorted colors)
- **Pocket Plant ID Tags** (plastic or bamboo, 50–100 pack)
- **Garden Trowel & Soil Thermometer** (stainless steel, simple dial)
- **Rain Gauge** (mountable or stake-style, 5-inch capacity)
- **Camera or Smartphone** (for photo-dating blooms and growth stages)
- **Weather-Proof Pen or Pencil Holder** (attachable to the journal or garden apron)
- **Seed Packet Storage Envelopes** (clear A5 or 4x6 inch sleeves)
- **Ruler or Measuring Tape** (12-inch folding ruler or 25-foot tape)

## Detailed Buying Guide

### Garden Journal Template
**Why this is your foundation.** Without a structured template, you’ll scribble random notes that become useless by next season. A dedicated template forces you to log: date planted, seed depth, first bloom date, last bloom date, and weather conditions. Look for templates that include pre-printed rows for 20–30 plants, a sun/shade indicator, and space for notes on pests or soil amendments. Digital templates (like Notion or Google Sheets) allow search and sort by bloom date; printable versions are better for gardeners who like physical writing. Avoid generic “blank journal” products—they lack the column headers you’ll rely on for consistency.

### Durable Binder or Spiral Notebook
**The physical home for your records.** A 1-inch ring binder with removable pages lets you add or rearrange seasonal sections (spring bulbs, summer perennials, fall veggies) without tearing pages. A spiral notebook (500+ pages) is cheaper but locked into one sequence—okay if you plan to start fresh yearly. Choose paper that’s heavy enough (90 gsm or more) to handle pen ink and water splashes. Coil binding lays flat for writing outdoors. Avoid glued spines; they break after a season of use.

### Permanent Markers
**Fading is the enemy of a garden log.** Sun, rain, and soil degrade cheap markers within weeks. Invest in archival-quality, waterproof, UV-resistant markers (e.g., Sakura Pigma Micron or Sharpie Extreme). Fine-tip (0.5–1mm) is essential for writing on plant tags and in tight journal cells. Black for basic data; red or blue for bloom dates. Test on a sample tag—if it smudges after 24 hours of rain, return it.

### Pocket Plant ID Tags
**Your journal’s outdoor counterpart.** When you plant a rose or tomato, you insert a tag next to it with the name and planting date. This bridges the gap between your journal (on the kitchen table) and the actual garden bed. Plastic tags last 2–3 seasons; bamboo biodegrades in one season but is eco-friendly. Buy in bulk (50+) because tags get lost, broken, or eaten by rodents. Opt for white blanks—colored tags fade unevenly.

### Garden Trowel & Soil Thermometer
**Precision planting starts at the soil level.** A trowel with depth markings (every 1 inch) lets you measure seed-hole depth accurately—a key factor noted in your journal. A soil thermometer (4–8 inch stem) ensures you plant warm-season crops (tomatoes, peppers) only when soil reaches 60°F or above. Logging soil temperature next to planting dates helps you predict bloom delays. Choose stainless steel over plastic; it lasts decades and won’t leach chemicals.

### Rain Gauge
**Water records unlock bloom timing.** Your journal’s “weather notes” column is useless without actual rainfall data. A rain gauge mounted on a post near the garden tells you how much water you *didn’t* need to add. Look for a gauge with a funnel top to minimize evaporation and easy-to-read inch/mm markings. The 5-inch capacity covers most weekly totals. Empty it after each reading and log the amount in your journal alongside bloom dates—dry spells correlate with delayed blooms.

### Camera or Smartphone
**Visual evidence beats memory.** A photo taken on the first bloom day gives you the exact color, petal count, and plant health—details words can miss. Use your smartphone’s date-stamp feature, or manually note the photo number in your journal. For serious trackers, a compact digital camera (with macro lens) captures fine details like pest damage or pollen color. Sync photos to a digital folder named by season (e.g., “2025_Spring_Blooms”).

### Weather-Proof Pen or Pencil Holder
**You will write in the rain.** Even if you “know” the forecast, mist from sprinklers or dew wets pages. A pen holder that clips to your journal’s cover or onto your apron ensures you always have a writing tool. Choose a waterproof mechanical pencil (no smearing) or a ballpoint pen with permanent ink (e.g., Fisher Space Pen). Avoid gel pens—they bleed when damp. The holder itself should be metal or heavy plastic to survive drops on concrete.

### Seed Packet Storage Envelopes
**Keep plant instructions linked to your logs.** Each seed packet contains depth, spacing, and days-to-bloom data you’ll need for journal entries. Bulky packets won’t fit in a standard journal pocket. Buy clear A5 envelopes (self-sealing) that hold 5–10 packets each. Label them by plant category (e.g., “Annual Flowers 2025”) and store them in a dedicated journal sleeve. This prevents the “where did I put the petunia info?” scramble every spring.

### Ruler or Measuring Tape
**Spacing accuracy affects bloom crowding.** When you log “spacing 12 inches” in your journal, you need to measure that at planting time. A 12-inch folding ruler fits in a garden apron pocket; a 25-foot tape is better for spacing rows or beds. Choose one with both inches and centimeters—some seed packets use metric. Mark your ruler with a permanent dot at common depths (¼, ½, 1 inch) for quick reference next to the trowel.
