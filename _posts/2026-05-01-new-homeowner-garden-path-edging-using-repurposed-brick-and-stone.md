## Essential Shopping List for a Repurposed Brick & Stone Garden Path

- **Repurposed bricks** (solid, non-flaking, from demolition sites or salvage yards)
- **Repurposed stone** (flagstone, cobbles, or fieldstone – irregular or uniform, depending on desired look)
- **Landscape fabric** (heavy-duty, permeable)
- **Edging stakes** or **metal landscape edging** (optional, for high-traffic areas)
- **Crushed gravel or paver base** (3/4-inch minus, for drainage and stabilization)
- **Coarse sand or stone dust** (for bedding and leveling)
- **Rubber mallet or dead-blow hammer** (for setting stones)
- **Spade or trenching shovel**
- **Tamper or hand compactor** (for soil/base compaction)
- **Level** (4-foot or torpedo)
- **String line and stakes** (for straightness)
- **Gloves** (heavy-duty, cut-resistant)
- **Wheelbarrow** (for moving materials)

## Buying Guide: The Logic Behind Each Item

### ## Repurposed Bricks

**Why repurpose?** New bricks are uniform, expensive, and lack the weathered patina that blends into an established garden. Repurposed bricks from demolition sites, architectural salvage yards, or even Craigslist offer character, lower cost, and environmental benefits. **Look for solid, hard-fired bricks** – avoid soft, crumbly, or “salmon” bricks (underfired) that will disintegrate after freeze-thaw cycles. Common types: red common, Chicago, or vintage “Roman” bricks. Test a few by tapping them together – a clear ring means high density; a dull thud indicates porosity.

### ## Repurposed Stone

**Flagstone** (bluestone, sandstone, limestone) provides flat, walkable surfaces. **Fieldstone or cobble** works better for informal or rustic edges. **Buying tip:** Always inspect stones in person for thickness – at least 1.5 inches for pedestrian traffic, 2-3 inches for vehicle edges. Avoid stones with hairline cracks or moss-covered surfaces (they may be too weathered). Mixing brick with stone creates visual contrast: use brick for low, clean borders and stone for irregular, naturalistic corners or steps.

### ## Landscape Fabric

This is **non-negotiable**. It prevents weeds from pushing up between bricks/stones while allowing water to drain. **Choose heavy-duty (4-6 oz per square yard) polypropylene fabric** – not cheap, thin weed barrier that tears under gravel weight. Longevity matters: higher denier fabrics last 10+ years while cheaper versions degrade in 1-2 seasons. Avoid black plastic sheeting, which traps moisture and rots organic matter beneath.

### ## Edging Stakes or Metal Landscape Edging

**Not always required**, but critical if your path borders a lawn (to prevent grass invasion) or receives foot traffic. **Steel stakes** (18-24 inches) driven into the ground on the outside of the bricks hold the structure against lateral movement. **Flexible metal edging** (galvanized steel or powder-coated aluminum) creates a crisp, low-profile barrier that keeps gravel and bricks in place. Avoid plastic edging – it cracks in cold weather and degrades in UV light.

### ## Crushed Gravel or Paver Base

This is the **foundation**. Gravel (3/4-inch minus) locks together when compacted, providing drainage and preventing frost heave. **Why not just sand?** Soil expands and contracts with moisture; gravel’s angular particles won’t shift as much. Buy “road base” or “Class 2 base” from a landscape supply yard – bagged products from big-box stores are overpriced for large paths. **Rule of thumb:** 2-3 inches of compacted gravel for light foot traffic, 4-6 inches for heavy use.

### ## Coarse Sand or Stone Dust

Sand fills gaps between bricks/stones, locking them in place. **Concrete sand** (coarse, angular) is superior to play sand because it doesn’t wash away as easily. **Stone dust** (quarry screenings) compacts into a near-solid layer but is messier to work with. **Why this layer?** Bricks laid directly on gravel will wobble; sand/stone dust creates a level, compressible bed that conforms to brick bottoms.

### ## Rubber Mallet or Dead-Blow Hammer

Essential for **setting bricks/stones without cracking them** – metal hammers will shatter brick. A **rubber mallet** works for most bricks; a **dead-blow hammer** (filled with metal shot) won’t bounce, reducing effort when setting heavy stone. Look for a 2-3 pound head for controlled tapping.

### ## Spade or Trenching Shovel

You need to dig a 4-6 inch trench for the base material. A standard spade is too wide – a **trenching shovel** (narrow, pointed blade) cuts clean edges. For curved paths, a **half-moon edger** (manual sod cutter) helps define the border. **Don’t skimp** – cheap shovels bend under heavy soil.

### ## Tamper or Hand Compactor

Uncompacted base material will settle unevenly, causing your path to sink. A **hand tamper** (steel plate on a long handle) works for small areas. For larger paths, rent a **plate compactor** ($35-$50/day) – the vibration compacts gravel 3x better than manual effort. **Tip:** Compact in 2-inch lifts (layers) for maximum density.

### ## Level

**A 4-foot level** checks that your path slopes away from structures (1/4 inch per foot) for drainage. A **torpedo level** is useful for individual bricks. **Logic:** Uneven bricks collect water, freeze, shift, and become tripping hazards.

### ## String Line and Stakes

Straight lines create visual order. Drive stakes at path ends, tie string taught a few inches above ground, and level the string using a line level. **Why?** Eye-level estimation fails over 10+ feet – the string is your reference for consistent height and alignment.

### ## Gloves

Repurposed bricks often have sharp edges from demolition; stone can be abrasive. **Leather-palmed, cut-resistant gloves** (Level 5 cut resistance) protect against cuts and blisters. Cotton gloves will shred within an hour.

### ## Wheelbarrow

Moving 500+ pounds of brick, stone, gravel, and sand by hand is exhausting and inefficient. A **contractor-grade wheelbarrow** (6+ cubic feet capacity, flat-free tire) saves time. Avoid garden-center “decorative” wheelbarrows with small wheels that sink into soil.

### Bonus: Do I Need Any Power Tools?

For small garden paths (under 50 linear feet), hand tools suffice. For longer runs, consider renting:
- **Angle grinder with a diamond blade** – for cutting bricks or stone to fit curves. Always wear goggles and a dust mask.
- **Plate compactor** – mentioned above; worth every penny for large areas.
