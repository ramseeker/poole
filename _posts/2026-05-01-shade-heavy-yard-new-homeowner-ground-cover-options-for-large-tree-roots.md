## Essential Shopping List for Shade-Heavy Yards with Large Tree Roots

- **Shade-Tolerant Ground Cover Plugs (e.g., *Pachysandra terminalis*, *Ajuga reptans*, *Hosta* varieties)**
- **Coarse-Grade Compost (organic, aged — not fresh manure or peat moss)**
- **Root Barrier Fabric (non-woven geotextile, permeability >30 gal/min/ft²)**
- **Granular Slow-Release Fertilizer (5-10-5 N-P-K or low-nitrogen formulation)**
- **Jute Erosion Control Blankets (biodegradable, open-weave)**
- **Heavy-Duty Irrigation Soaker Hose (3/4” diameter, porous rubber)**
- **Landscape Staples (10-gauge, 6” length for deep-rooted anchoring)**
- **Hand Trowel with Depth Gauge (for planting between roots)**
- **Top Dressing of Fine-Grade Bark Mulch (≤1” particle size, non-dyed)**
- **Grass-Safe Soil Acidifier (sulfur or iron sulfate, if soil pH is above 6.5)**

## Buying Guide: Logic for Each Item

### Shade-Tolerant Ground Cover Plugs
**Why it matters:** Large tree roots create intense competition for water and nutrients, plus dense shade prevents most turf grass from surviving. Plugs of proven shade performers like *Pachysandra* (Japanese spurge) spread via rhizomes, forming a living mulch that withstands root stress. Choose plugs over seeds because established plants have a root system that can anchor into the thin topsoil between roots. Avoid *Vinca minor* (periwinkle) if you want low maintenance — it can smother native plants and be invasive in warmer climates.

### Coarse-Grade Compost
**The logic:** Tree roots near the surface deprive ground covers of organic matter. Standard garden soils compact under root pressure. **Coarse compost** improves drainage and aeration without smothering roots. Fine compost or peat moss can hold too much moisture around tree roots, promoting fungal diseases like root rot. Aged mushroom compost works well because it has a neutral pH and low salt content. Add only a 1-inch layer — never dig more than 3 inches deep around mature trees to avoid damaging feeder roots.

### Root Barrier Fabric (Non-Woven Geotextile)
**Why this is non-negotiable:** Thin, woven landscape fabric (often sold as weed barrier) blocks water and air, eventually killing ground covers. **Non-woven geotextile** allows water, oxygen, and fine roots to pass through while physically blocking tree roots from bulging up and displacing your new plants. Use it under walkways or as a buffer around tree bases. The permeability rating *must exceed 30 gallons per minute per square foot* — check the product label. Avoid black plastic or rubber mats, which bake soil and suffocate ground covers.

### Granular Slow-Release Fertilizer (5-10-5 or Low-Nitrogen)
**The logic:** High-nitrogen fertilizers (e.g., 30-0-0) encourage leafy growth but stress tree roots by forcing them to compete for water. A **low-nitrogen, slow-release formula** like 5-10-5 supports root development for ground covers without overstimulating tree growth. Apply only after planting and during early spring. Do NOT fertilize within 3 feet of the tree trunk — roots there are most sensitive to chemical burns.

### Jute Erosion Control Blankets
**Why they’re essential here:** Large tree roots create uneven ground that traps rainwater, causing erosion and washing away fine soil. **Open-weave jute blankets** stabilize soil on slopes or root-knots without blocking sunlight. They degrade naturally within 6–12 months, allowing ground covers to root through. Synthetic nets (e.g., polypropylene) can strangle plants and entangle wildlife. Use 6” landscape staples to secure blankets at every root ridge.

### Heavy-Duty Irrigation Soaker Hose (3/4” Diameter)
**The logic:** Tree roots steal water from the soil surface. Standard drip lines get tangled on protruding roots and clog easily. A **3/4” thick soaker hose** with porous rubber (not polyethylene tubing) delivers water slowly over hours, wetting the top 4–6 inches of soil where ground covers live. Thread it in serpentine patterns around root bases — not across them — to avoid kinking. Pair with a pressure regulator (10–15 PSI) to prevent hose bursts.

### Landscape Staples (10-Gauge, 6” Length)
**Why size matters:** Short staples (4” or less) pop out when tree roots expand. **6-inch, 10-gauge staples** penetrate through root zones and into subsoil. Use them to secure jute blankets and soaker hoses. Gold-colored staples are inferior to zinc-coated steel — the latter resist rust from constant moisture. Count on 12–15 staples per 10-foot length of blanket or hose.

### Hand Trowel with Depth Gauge
**The logic:** You’ll need to dig planting holes that are *precisely 2–3 inches deep* — deeper holes damage tree roots and create air pockets. A trowel with a calibrated depth gauge (notched at 2” and 4”) prevents accidental root slicing. Avoid using a shovel, which can sever critical tree roots as thick as a finger. Look for a forged stainless steel blade; cheap carbon steel bends easily in root-filled soil.

### Top Dressing of Fine-Grade Bark Mulch (≤1” Particle Size)
**Why coarse mulch fails:** Large bark nuggets (2–4”) pile up around tree roots, trapping moisture against bark and inviting rot. **Fine-grade bark mulch** (≤1” particles) decomposes quickly, adding organic matter without smothering ground cover crowns. Use only 1–2 inches — deeper than that suffocates ground cover roots. Avoid dyed mulches (red, black, brown) because artificial colors often contain heavy metals that leach into soil and harm beneficial fungi.

### Grass-Safe Soil Acidifier (Sulfur or Iron Sulfate)
**The logic:** Shade-loving ground covers like *Pachysandra* and *Hosta* prefer acidic soil (pH 5.0–6.5). Tree leaf litter (especially maple, oak, and birch) can make soil alkaline over time. Test soil pH first with a home kit. If pH exceeds 6.5, **granular sulfur** (not powdered) is slow-acting but safest. Iron sulfate works faster but stains concrete and clothing. Do **not** use aluminum sulfate — it contains toxic aluminum that kills tree roots and accumulates in groundwater.
