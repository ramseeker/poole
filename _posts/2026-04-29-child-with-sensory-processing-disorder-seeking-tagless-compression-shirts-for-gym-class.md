## Detailed Buying Guide: Why Each Item Matters

### **Tagless Compression Shirt (Long-Sleeve)**  
**Purpose:** Primary gym-class wear for deep pressure input and core temperature regulation.  
**Why it works:** Long-sleeve compression shirts provide full-arms proprioceptive input, which helps calm an overstimulated nervous system. The tagless design eliminates the most common tactile trigger—scratchy labels rubbing against the back of the neck or shoulder blades. Look for shirts labeled “sensory-friendly” or “seamless” from brands like **Under Armour’s HeatGear Tagless**, **Kozie Clothes’ sensory compression tops**, or **Target’s Cat & Jack Sensory Friendly line**. **Size up slightly** if tightness feels restrictive; the goal is firm but not painful.

### **Tagless Compression Shirt (Short-Sleeve)**  
**Purpose:** Versatile for warmer days or as a base layer under other gym clothing.  
**Why it works:** Short-sleeve options reduce the amount of fabric-touch on the arms, which can be overwhelming for kids with tactile defensiveness. The tagless feature again prevents direct irritation. Prioritize **moisture-wicking fabrics** (polyester-spandex blends) to prevent sweat cling, which can trigger sensory overload. Check that the **neckline is seamless** (no binding) and the hem doesn’t have a raised elastic band.

### **Flat-Seam or Seamless Compression Shirt**  
**Purpose:** Eliminates all internal raised seams that cause rubbing and chafing.  
**Why it works:** Standard compression shirts often have flatlocked seams that still protrude internally. For a child with SPD, even a 1mm ridge can feel like a sharp line. **Seamless compression tops** (knit in a single tube with no side seams) are ideal. If seamless isn’t available, look for “flat-seam construction” where seams are sewn flat against the fabric. Brands like **Sensiband**, **Kutchina**, or **Boody** specialize in seam-free designs. **Test the inside seam with your finger** before buying—run it across the armpit and shoulder areas.

### **Sensory-Friendly Fabric-Finish Shirt**  
**Purpose:** Provides a non-abrasive interior texture that feels soft, not silky or slippery.  
**Why it works:** Many compression shirts have a slick, shiny finish that can feel uncomfortable against sensitive skin. Some kids prefer a **brushed-back, cotton-blend interior** (e.g., modal or bamboo), which mimics a soft T-shirt feel while retaining compression. Brands like **Free Label**, **Sensory Goods**, or **The Sensory Shop** offer compression with a “peach finish” or micro-fleece lining. **Avoid “thermal” fabrics** with heavy ribbing—they can feel bumpy.

### **Tagless Compression Tank Top**  
**Purpose:** Good for high-movement activities (running, jumping) or when the child needs less arm coverage.  
**Why it works:** Tanks reduce total fabric contact, which can be less overwhelming for a child who struggles with body-awareness under layers. The tagless feature prevents neckline irritation. Look for a **racerback style** (wider straps stay in place) and **no side seams**. This can also serve as a cool-down shirt post-gym when the child needs to shed layers.

### **Silicone or Rubber Grip Patches (optional)**  
**Purpose:** Provides a small, safe tactile target for self-regulation during class.  
**Why it works:** Some children with SPD seek extra sensory input (proprioceptive or tactile). Silicone patches sewn onto the wrist, shoulder, or lower back of a compression shirt (on the *outside* only) give the child a discreet object to press, pinch, or rub during high-anxiety moments. **Sew-on only**—adhesive patches can peel off and cause distraction. Look for **non-slip, food-grade silicone patches** (like those used for yoga socks).

### **Laundry-Safe Tag-Removal Tool**  
**Purpose:** For shirts that come with tags that cannot be removed by hand.  
**Why it works:** Even “tagless” shirts sometimes have printed-on care labels that feel stiff. A **seam ripper** or **small fabric snips** allow you to remove the entire sewn-in tag without damaging the shirt. **Never use scissors**—they can leave a sharp edge. **Test after removal**: rub the area with your palm. If it’s still rough, apply a small **satin patch** or **cotton patch** over the spot.

### **Final Buying Tips**  
- **Fit first:** Compression should be snug, not tight. Measure the child’s chest, waist, and arm length. If between sizes, **size up**—a shirt too tight can cause panic.  
- **Fabric care:** Wash all new shirts **twice** before wearing to remove manufacturing chemicals. Use **free-and-clear detergent** to avoid fragrance sensitivity.  
- **Test at home:** Have the child wear the shirt for 15 minutes during a calm activity (e.g., reading). If they ask to remove it, try a different style.
