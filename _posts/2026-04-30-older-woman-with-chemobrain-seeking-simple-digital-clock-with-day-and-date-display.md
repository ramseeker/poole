## Shopping List: Simple Digital Clock for Chemo Brain (Senior-Friendly)

- **Primary Clock:** Large-digit digital clock with automatic time setting (atomic or Wi-Fi).
- **Power Source:** Hardwired or plug-in model with battery backup (no batteries as main power).
- **Display Type:** High-contrast LCD or LED (non-glare, wide viewing angle).
- **Day and Date:** Simultaneous display of day of week (spelled out, not abbreviated) and large date.
- **Button Layout:** Minimal buttons (fewer than 3) with tactile feedback (or no-button touch/sensor).
- **Night Mode:** Adjustable brightness dimmer (not auto-dimming that can be confusing).
- **Alarm:** Optional, but if present, must have simple on/off toggle and single-button snooze.
- **Mounting Option:** Wall-mountable or sturdy table stand with non-slip base.

## Buying Guide

### Why Automation Matters: Atomic or Wi-Fi Time Setting
Chemo brain can make setting or resetting a clock after a power outage deeply frustrating. **Choose a clock that sets itself automatically.** Atomic clocks sync with the US atomic time signal (WWVB) or use Wi-Fi to pull time from the internet. This eliminates the need to navigate menus for Daylight Saving Time changes or battery replacements.

- **Atomic clocks** are ideal for rural areas without reliable Wi-Fi. They update daily and require zero user input.
- **Wi-Fi clocks** (e.g., models by La Crosse Technology or Marathon) sync online but may need initial Wi-Fi setup via a smartphone app—**avoid this** if you are not comfortable with phone pairing. For simplicity, **choose a pure atomic clock** that works out of the box.

### Power Backup: The Hidden Frustration
A clock that loses time or settings during a power outage is useless. **Look for a clock with a built-in battery backup** (often a single CR2032 coin cell) that only preserves the time and date memory, not the display. The display should automatically relight when power returns. **Never use a clock that requires multiple AA batteries as its primary power source**—these die unpredictably and require dexterity to replace.

- **Tip:** Check that the backup battery is user-replaceable without tools. Avoid models with soldered-in batteries.

### Display Design: Beyond "Big Numbers"
"Large digits" is not enough for someone with chemobrain. The clock must display the **day of the week spelled out** (e.g., "Monday") rather than "MON" or "1". Abbreviations require mental translation, which strains cognitive load. The **date should be prominently sized**—at least 50% the height of the time digits.

- **Contrast is critical:** White digits on a black background offer the highest readability for aging eyes and reduce glare. Avoid metallic or colored digits (e.g., blue on silver) that wash out in low light.
- **Viewing angle:** The display should remain legible from 45 degrees off-center, as you may see it while lying down or from across the room. LCD screens are generally better than LED for this, but high-quality LED (e.g., La Crosse C84388) also works.
- **Anti-glare:** Check reviews for "reflective screen" complaints. A matte-finished lens or a clock with a recessed display helps.

### Minimalist Interface: Fighting Button Overload
Chemo brain often causes confusion with complex settings. **The ideal clock has no more than three buttons** (e.g., "Set," " Up," " Down") or a single "Mode" button. **Even better:** a clock with a large touch-sensitive top (like the Marathon Jumbo Atomic Clock) that changes display brightness or snoozes alarm with a single tap. Avoid clocks with 5+ side buttons, as they lead to accidental presses and menu traps.

- **Alarm simplicity:** If you choose a model with an alarm, it must have a single "Alarm On/Off" switch (not a multi-step menu) and a large snooze bar. **Do not buy** a clock that requires holding a button for 3 seconds to dismiss the alarm—this is cognitively demanding.

### Night Mode: Predictability Over Features
Auto-dimming clocks adjust brightness based on room light—this can be disorienting when you glance at the clock from a dark room and see nothing. **Select a clock with a manual brightness dimmer** (e.g., low/medium/high switch) or one that stays at a constant, low brightness. A soft, amber-tinted LED (rather than blue or green) is less likely to disrupt sleep and is easier on aging eyes.

- **Avoid** clocks that completely black out at night unless you manually set a timer—this adds unnecessary steps.

### Mounting & Placement: Reducing Visual Clutter
A clock that falls over or is placed too far from the bed adds cognitive friction. **Choose a clock that can be wall-mounted** (standardized screw holes) or sits on a non-slip stand. The display should be at **eye level from your bedside or favorite chair**—mount it so you don’t have to tilt your head or squint. For bedside use, a slightly reclined stand (like a 15-degree tilt) reduces glare.

- **Size:** Opt for a display width of at least 5 inches for digits. A smaller clock defeats the purpose of easier readability. Example: The **La Crosse Technology 513-1419** has 1.5-inch digits and is wall-mountable, but the **Marathon CL030060** offers 3-inch digits for easier reading.

### Recommended Final Check: Model Examples That Fit These Criteria
- **Best Overall:** La Crosse Technology C84388 (Atomic, 3-inch digits, single snooze bar, manual dimmer, wall-mountable, CR2032 backup). Avoid the Wi-Fi version for simplicity.
- **Budget Option:** Marathon Jumbo Atomic Clock (2.5-inch digits, touch-sensor dimmer, simple alarm, no app required).
- **Ultra-Simple:** Sharp Atomic Clock SPC935 (No alarm, 2-inch digits, single button for brightness, intuitive setup).

**Test before buying:** Read product reviews for phrases like "easy to set," "no manual needed," or "grandma could use it." Avoid any clock with user complaints about confusing menus or complicated setup processes.
