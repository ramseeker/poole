## Essential Shopping List for New Homeowner Raised Bed Pest Control (Insect Netting & Row Covers)

- **Insect Netting** (fine mesh, 0.6mm or smaller)
- **Row Cover Fabric** (lightweight, 0.5–1.0 oz/sq yd)
- **Support Hoops** (metal or PVC, 14–16 gauge)
- **Hoops Clips or Clamps** (snap-on type, UV-resistant)
- **Netting/Row Cover Weights or Sandbags** (1–2 lb each, 4–6 per bed)
- **Scissors or Utility Knife** (sharp, for cutting materials)
- **Staple Gun & Staples** (for attaching netting to wooden frames, optional)
- **Garden Twine or Elastic Cord** (for tying hoops to bed edges)
- **Spare PVC Pipe or Rebar Stakes** (for adding height or wind stability)
- **Zippered Insect Net Rack** (pre-assembled, for easy access – optional but recommended)

## Detailed Buying Guide

### ## Insect Netting (Fine Mesh, ~0.6mm or Smaller)
**Why it’s essential:** This is your primary physical barrier against flying pests like aphids, whiteflies, cabbage moths, and leafminers. The mesh size is critical: 0.6mm (or 0.8mm) stops most small insects while still allowing sunlight and rain penetration. Look for **UV-stabilized polyethylene** or **polypropylene** to prevent degradation under sun exposure. Avoid cheap, low-density netting that tears easily; invest in a **13–20% shade factor** netting—too high a shade factor can stunt heat-loving crops like tomatoes or peppers.

### ## Row Cover Fabric (Lightweight, 0.5–1.0 oz/sq yd)
**Why it’s essential:** Unlike netting, row covers are made from spun-bonded polyester or polypropylene that blocks up to 85% of light but allows air and water permeability. They are ideal for **cool-season crops** (lettuce, spinach, brassicas) where you need frost protection (2–4°F buffer) and pest exclusion. The lighter weight (0.5 oz) is for insect control and light frost; heavier (1.0 oz) works for moderate frost and wind. **Never use solid plastic**—it will cook plants in direct sun. Buy a **pre-cut, grommeted cover** for small beds or a roll for custom sizes.

### ## Support Hoops (Metal or PVC, 14–16 gauge)
**Why they matter:** Both netting and row covers must be lifted above foliage to prevent direct contact, which can cause leaf burn (from trapped heat) or pest access (if fabric lies flat). **Metal hoops** (galvanized steel, often 14-gauge) are durable and won’t snap in wind, but may rust over time. **PVC hoops** (1/2-inch diameter, schedule 40) are lightweight and cheap, but can bend in heavy snow. Choose **14-gauge metal hoops** for permanent beds, or **PVC with reinforcement stakes** if you move hoops seasonally. The arch height should be at least 6–12 inches taller than your tallest crop (e.g., tomatoes need 24-inch hoops).

### ## Hoop Clips or Clamps (Snap-On, UV-Resistant)
**Why they’re non-negotiable:** These secure netting or row cover to the hoops. The **snap-on type** (e.g., “SmartClips” or “Tunnel Clips”) allow you to quickly lift material for watering or harvesting without full removal. Avoid zip ties—they pinch fabric and break under UV. Look for **polypropylene or UV-stabilized nylon** clips that fit your hoop diameter (common sizes: 1/2-inch or 3/4-inch). Budget for at least 2–3 clips per hoop per side.

### ## Netting/Row Cover Weights or Sandbags (1–2 lb Each)
**Why you need them:** The bottom edges of netting and row covers must be **completely sealed** against the soil surface. Loose edges let pests crawl under. Use **1-lb fabric sandbags** (sewn, with filler) or **rubber-capped lead weights** (3–5 per 4x8-foot bed). Avoid rocks—they leave gaps and crush plants. For long-term beds, **board clips** or **landscape fabric pins** hold material to wooden bed sides.

### ## Scissors or Utility Knife (Sharp)
**Why it’s specific:** You’ll need to cut netting and fabric to the exact bed size, plus trim corners for corners. Dull blades fray edges, weakening the material over time. Use **bypass scissors** (for clean cuts on fabric) or a **heavy-duty utility knife** with a fresh blade for thick polypropylene netting. **Pro tip:** Cut netting 12–18 inches wider than the bed on all sides to allow for anchoring.

### ## Staple Gun & Staples (For Wooden Frames, Optional)
**Why you might want it:** If your raised bed has wooden sides (rather than metal or plastic), you can staple netting directly to the frame for a **permanent, gap-free seal**. Use **1/4-inch or 3/8-inch staples** in a pneumatic or electric stapler. This is ideal for long-term insect exclusion but makes access harder—compatible only if you install a **zippered door** or lift the whole frame.

### ## Garden Twine or Elastic Cord (For Tying Hoops to Bed Edges)
**Why it’s useful:** High winds can lift hoops off the bed, toppling the tunnel. **Elastic bungee cord** (1/4-inch diameter) stretches enough to secure hoops to eye hooks or bed corners without ripping fabric. Standard garden twine works but degrades quickly; choose **polypropylene bungee cords** with hooks for easy on/off.

### ## Spare PVC Pipe or Rebar Stakes (For Adding Height or Wind Stability)
**Why you should keep some on hand:** Row covers and netting can sag in rain or snow. Insert **18-inch rebar stakes** into the ground at each hoop base to anchor them. Or use **vertical PVC pipes** (1/2-inch, 3 feet tall) as central posts for tall crops like sunflowers or pole beans. This prevents the netting from flattening against plants.

### ## Zippered Insect Net Rack (Pre-Assembled, Recommended)
**Why this is a game-changer:** Instead of DIY hoops and clips, you can buy a **complete pop-up tunnel** with integrated zippered ends. These are pricier ($30–$70 for a 2x4-foot bed) but save assembly time. Look for **heavy-duty metal frames** with **self-sealing zippers** (anti-jam, weather-resistant). They allow easy access without lifting the whole net—critical for daily harvesting. **Warning:** Ensure the zipper is at least 8 inches off the ground to prevent soil clogging.
