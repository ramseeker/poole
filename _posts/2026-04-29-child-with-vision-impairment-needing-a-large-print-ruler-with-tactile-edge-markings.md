## Essential Shopping List

- **Large-Print Ruler (12-inch or 30-cm)** with tactile edge markings (raised or embossed numbers)
- **Tactile measuring triangle** (for angle work and geometry)
- **High-contrast non-slip ruler mat** or **tacky ruler grip strip**
- **Portable magnifier bar** (optional, for double-checking fine increments)
- **Durable storage case** (hard plastic with compartments)

---

## ## Buying Guide: Why Each Item Matters

### **Large-Print Ruler with Tactile Edge Markings**

**Logic:** A child with vision impairment needs to *feel* the markings as much as read them. Standard rulers have smooth edges and tiny, low-contrast numbers—frustrating even for partial vision. Look for:
- **Raised or embossed markings** that run along the ruler’s edge. The child can slide a finger along the ruler to locate the measurement without relying solely on sight.
- **High-contrast colors** (white or yellow numbers on a dark ruler body, or black on bright yellow). Avoid grayscale rulers with poor contrast.
- **Large print** (minimum 18–24 point font) for each inch/cm increment. Ensure the numbering is bold, not italic or thin.
- **Tactile notches** at 1-inch or 1-cm intervals for quick finger-referencing. Some rulers have a small raised dot at each increment.
- **Material:** Hard plastic or metal (avoid flexible plastic, which can warp and confuse measurements). Metal rulers with a curved, beveled edge are easiest for tactile tracking.

### **Tactile Measuring Triangle**

**Logic:** Geometry often requires measuring angles and drawing perpendicular lines. A standard plastic triangle is slippery and has tiny, glossy numbers.
- **Features:** Raised angle markers (e.g., 30°, 60°, 90°) and a lip or groove along the straight edge so the child can align it by touch.
- **Size:** At least 8 inches along the hypotenuse—too small and the child loses fine motor control.
- **Contrast:** Same high-contrast principle as the ruler—bright body with dark, large-print numbers.

### **High-Contrast Non-Slip Ruler Mat or Tacky Ruler Grip Strip**

**Logic:** A ruler that slides around on paper is useless for a child with vision impairment. They need to keep the ruler stable while their other hand traces the edge.
- **Option A: Self-adhesive ruler grips** (thin silicone strips with rubberized backs). Stick one along the underside of the ruler—it prevents shifting on paper or desks.
- **Option B: Portable non-slip mat** (like a kitchen drawer liner cut to size). Place it under the paper; the ruler’s weight and texture grip it well. Choose a bright yellow or orange mat for visual contrast against white paper.
- **Why it matters:** Stability reduces frustration and increases accuracy, especially for drawing lines or measuring width.

### **Portable Magnifier Bar**

**Logic:** Even with large print, small sub-increments (e.g., 1/16th or 1 mm marks) may still be too tiny to see clearly. A magnifier bar is a convenient backup.
- **Style:** A rectangular, flat magnifier that rests directly on the ruler (no handle to juggle). Look for **2x to 3x magnification**—higher power can distort numbers.
- **Built-in LED light** is helpful in low-light classrooms or uneven lighting.
- **Note:** This is optional—only if the child has partial sight and occasionally needs to verify a tiny marking. For total blindness, rely solely on tactile markings.

### **Durable Storage Case**

**Logic:** Tactile rulers are precision tools—bent edges, scratched surfaces, or lost markings make them unusable.
- **Hard case** (e.g., plastic pencil box) with foam inserts to keep the ruler from sliding around. Soft cases or cloth pouches offer less protection.
- **Labeled compartments:** Store the ruler, triangle, magnifier, and grip strips separately. A child who cannot see well must be able to find the correct tool by touch alone—use high-contrast fabric labels (bright red or yellow) on each compartment lid.
- **Portability:** A case that fits in a backpack or desk without crushing the ruler’s raised markings.
