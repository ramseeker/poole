## Essential Shopping List for Cardboard Mulch Garden Beds

- **Corrugated cardboard (large sheets, no tape/labels/glossy coating)**
- **Heavy-duty utility knife or box cutter with extra blades**
- **Garden hose with spray nozzle or 5-gallon bucket**
- **Organic compost (1–2 cubic yards per 100 sq. ft.)**
- **Wood chip mulch or shredded bark (2–3 cubic yards per 100 sq. ft.)**
- **Landscape fabric pins or earth staples (6–8 inch length)**
- **Gardening gloves (waterproof or rubber-coated)**
- **Safety glasses**
- **Long-handled rake or garden fork**
- **Measuring tape or garden line**

## Detailed Buying Guide

### ## Cardboard: The Star of the Show

**Why it matters:** Cardboard is the weed barrier that decomposes, feeds soil microbes, and costs almost nothing. Avoid shiny, coated, or colored boxes—they contain plastic and harmful dyes.

**What to buy:**
- **Source:** Recycling bins, moving stores (ask for free flattened boxes), appliance stores. Avoid pizza boxes (grease attracts pests) or heavily printed boxes.
- **Thickness:** Double- or triple-wall corrugated works best; single-layer cardboard will degrade too quickly for a new bed.
- **Sheet size:** Aim for at least 3 ft. x 3 ft. sheets to minimize overlaps. Smaller pieces mean more pinning and potential gaps.

**Pro tip:** Lay out your cardboard *before* buying compost/mulch to measure exact square footage. Order 10% extra for overlaps and mistakes.

### ## Cutting Tools: Precision Without Frustration

**Why it matters:** Frayed, uneven cuts leave gaps for weeds to sprout. Dull blades tear cardboard and cause wrist strain.

**What to buy:**
- **Utility knife:** Look for a retractable, ergonomic model with a **snap-off blade** (e.g., OLFA 25mm or Stanley FatMax). A blade-dispenser knife allows instant sharpness without stopping to find replacements.
- **Extra blades:** Buy a 100-pack of **heavy-duty trapezoid blades**. Cardboard dulls blades fast; change after every 50 sq. ft. A sharp blade cuts cleanly through overlapping layers.
- **Safety glasses:** Essential when cutting overhead or near roots—cardboard dust and staple fragments can fly.

**Budget alternative:** A **serrated bread knife** can saw through cardboard without the need for a cutting mat, but it’s slower and less precise.

### ## Securing the Cardboard: Pins vs. Rocks

**Why it matters:** Unsecured cardboard will curl up, shift in wind, or get kicked by wildlife. Pins are cleaner than gathering rocks.

**What to buy:**
- **Landscape fabric pins (earth staples):** Choose **6–8 inch length** galvanized steel (avoid plastic—they snap in hard ground). Count on 1 pin per 2 sq. ft. of cardboard, plus extra at seams and corners.
- **Alternatives:** *Reusable tent stakes* work for soft soil; *bamboo skewers* (8–10 inch) are cheap but rot in a season. Avoid wooden pegs—they rot and attract termites near foundations.

**When to skip pins:** If you’re layering cardboard *under* heavy compost (4+ inches), you can use **thick rocks or bricks** on edges only. But for steep slopes or windy areas, pins are non-negotiable.

### ## Compost: The Weed-Killing Engine

**Why it matters:** Compost feeds your future plants while smothering existing weeds. The biology in high-quality compost outcompetes weed seeds in the soil.

**What to buy:**
- **Type:** *Screened* organic compost (avoid "topsoil" or "garden soil" which may contain weed seeds). Look for OMRI-certified if organically gardening.
- **Quantity:** For a new bed, apply **at least 3–4 inches**—this depth blocks light and provides a rich seedbed for transplants. A 1-yard bag covers ~100 sq. ft. at 3 inches deep.
- **Where to buy:** Bulk from local landscaping suppliers (costs ~$40–$60 per yard), not bagged from big-box stores (often 2x price). Ask if it’s *weed-free*—some commercial composts are made from municipal waste that includes weed seeds.

**Test before buying:** Grab a handful—it should smell earthy, not sour or ammonia-like. Squeeze it: water should barely drip out. If it's soggy, it’s anaerobic and can rot roots.

### ## Top Mulch: The Sunshield and Moisture Guard

**Why it matters:** Cardboard breaks down in sunlight; mulch protects it while retaining moisture for decomposer life.

**What to buy:**
- **Type:** Shredded hardwood bark or **pine bark nuggets** (stay in place best). Avoid fresh arborist chips—they contain green leaves that can breed fungi and initially rob nitrogen.
- **Depth:** **3–4 inches** on top of compost. Thinner on slopes (2 inches) to prevent sliding.
- **Quantity:** 1 cubic yard covers ~80 sq. ft. at 3 inches deep. Buy 10% extra for settling.
- **Cost:** Bulk shredded bark (~$35–$50 per yard) is cheaper than bagged. If bagged, use **non-dyed** mulch—dyed (red/black) mulches often contain construction wood waste and chemical stains.

**Fire safety note:** In dry climates, avoid fine-textured mulches (like sawdust) that can smolder. Use coarse bark or stone mulch (though stone doesn’t nourish soil).

### ## Watering Gear: Activating the Layer Cake

**Why it matters:** Cardboard is hydrophobic when dry; it repels water. You need to soak it thoroughly so it begins decomposing.

**What to buy:**
- **Garden hose with adjustable spray nozzle:** A **fan or shower pattern** works best—jet streams blast holes in cardboard.
- **Alternative:** A 5-gallon bucket with small holes drilled in the lid (like a makeshift watering can). Slower but more controlled for precise soaking.
- **Rain barrel (optional):** If you have downspout access, rainwater is free and avoids chlorine that slows microbial activity.

**Pro tip:** Wet the cardboard *before* adding compost—this prevents the compost from sliding off a dry, slick surface.

### ## Measuring Tools: Avoid Underbuying

**Why it matters:** Ordering too little compost/mulch mid-project wastes time and money. Underbuying by 20% means ugly thin patches.

**What to buy:**
- **Measuring tape:** A 50-foot reel is fine; 100-foot is overkill for most beds. Use it to mark your bed perimeter.
- **Garden line or string:** Helps create straight edges when cutting cardboard for borders. Use bright flagging tape (visible against cardboard).
- **Notebook/phone:** Sketch your bed layout with measurements. Multiply **length x width** = square footage. Then multiply by **0.25 (for 3 inches deep)** to get cubic yards needed (example: 200 sq. ft. x 0.25 = 50 cubic ft., divide by 27 = ~1.85 yards).

### ## Protective Gear: Your Health Matters

**Why it matters:** Cardboard dust, compost fungi, and staple shards are no joke. Gardener’s lung (aspergillosis) is real.

**What to buy:**
- **Gardening gloves:** Look for **nitrile-coated or rubber-palmed**—cotton gloves soak through when wet, and cardboard is rough. Extra-long cuffs prevent soil from running up your wrists.
- **Dust mask (N95):** Wear when cutting dry cardboard or handling dusty compost. Moldy cardboard releases spores.
- **Knee pads:** A low-cost foam pair saves your knees during three hours of pinning cardboard.

### ## Optional: Tools for Existing Weed Removal

**Why it might be needed:** If your site has stubborn perennial weeds (quackgrass, bindweed, blackberries), cardboard alone won’t stop them from growing sideways under the barrier.

**What to buy:**
- **Garden fork or broadfork:** Loosen soil *before* laying cardboard to encourage deep root decomposition. Use a **digging fork** (not a pitchfork) with flat tines.
- **Hand weeder (hori-hori knife):** For slicing out taproots of dandelions or thistles visible at the surface.
- **White vinegar (20% strength)** in a spray bottle: Spot-treat persistent weeds 2 weeks before laying cardboard. Avoid standard household vinegar (5%)—it’s too weak for roots.

**Advanced tip:** For heavy clay or compacted soil, rent a **manual sod cutter** to remove top 2 inches of turf—this replaces the need for a thick cardboard layer and ensures fewer weed regrowths.
