## Essential Shopping List

- **Bareroot or potted starter plants** (e.g., lavender, Russian sage, yarrow, sedum, coneflower, black-eyed Susan) – 3-5 varieties, 2-3 plants each for a 4x4 ft bed
- **Organic compost** (1 cubic foot per 10 sq ft of bed)
- **Coarse sand or small gravel** (for soil amending, 1 bag per 4 sq ft of clay soil)
- **Drip irrigation kit** (1/2-inch tubing, emitters, and a timer)
- **Landscape fabric or biodegradable mulch** (3-inch layer, e.g., shredded bark or straw)
- **Garden trowel** (stainless steel, ergonomic handle)
- **Soil pH test kit** (liquid or probe type)
- **Slow-release, low-nitrogen fertilizer** (e.g., 5-10-10 NPK ratio)
- **Gardening gloves** (leather or reinforced rubber)
- **Hand pruners** (bypass style, sharpened blades)

## Buying Guide: Why Each Item Matters

### Bareroot vs. Potted Starter Plants
**Bareroot plants** are dormant, sold without soil, and are often cheaper. They establish roots faster in drought-friendly gardens because they’re not acclimated to pot-specific soil. **Potted plants** offer instant visual impact but can suffer from root binding. **For a new homeowner**, choose potted 4-inch perennials for reliability—bareroot demands precise timing (early spring planting). Focus on *drought-tolerant natives* like coneflower (*Echinacea*) or lavender (*Lavandula*), which have deep taproots to access groundwater. Mix 3-5 varieties for bloom succession: early (yarrow), mid (Russian sage), late (sedum).

### Organic Compost
Compost is not filler—it’s a **water-retention sponge** and **microbial engine**. Sandy soil drains too fast; clay soil cakes into hardpan. Compost balances both by improving structure. For drought beds, mix 2-3 inches into the top 6-8 inches of soil. Skip manure-based compost (high nitrogen pushes leafy growth, stressing roots in dry periods). Look for “humus” or “garden compost” on the bag, free of synthetic additives.

### Coarse Sand or Small Gravel
Drought-tolerant plants hate waterlogged roots. If your soil is heavy clay (role a ball, it doesn’t crumble), add sand **at a 1:3 ratio with compost** to create fast-draining pockets. Use angular, coarse sand (not beach sand, which compacts). Gravel works for “dry garden” designs—lay a 1-inch layer under the planting hole to prevent root rot.

### Drip Irrigation Kit
Sprinklers waste water through evaporation; overhead watering coat leaves, inviting fungal disease. **Drip irrigation** delivers water directly to the root zone at a slow, deep pace—ideal for drought-tolerant plants that need infrequent but deep soaks. Buy a kit with a pressure regulator and a timer (set for 30 minutes every 3-5 days, adjusting for rain). Choose 1/2-inch tubing over soaker hoses, which can clog hard.

### Landscape Fabric vs. Biodegradable Mulch
**Landscape fabric** blocks weeds and holds moisture, but it’s non-biodegradable and can trap heat, burning roots. For a first garden, use **biodegradable mulch** (shredded bark, straw, or wood chips). A 3-inch layer reduces soil temperature by 10°F and cuts water loss by 50%. Avoid dyed mulches (contain heavy metals). Shredded hardwood breaks down slowly—reapply every 2 years.

### Garden Trowel
A cheap trowel bends under tough soil. Invest in a **stainless steel, forged trowel** with a rubberized handle. The blade should be narrow (for tight planting holes) and have measurement marks (for consistent depth). Avoid wooden handles—they rot. Use it for digging holes 1.5x the root ball width to reduce transplant shock.

### Soil pH Test Kit
Drought-tolerant plants like lavender, sage, and yarrow prefer **slightly alkaline soil** (pH 6.5-7.5). A test kit prevents waste: if your soil is acidic (under pH 6.0), add pulverized lime (1 cup per 10 sq ft) to raise it. Liquid kits are cheap and accurate; probe meters are quicker but need calibration. Test after mixing in compost.

### Low-Nitrogen Fertilizer (5-10-10)
High-nitrogen fertilizer (like 30-0-0) encourages soft, leafy growth that collapses under drought stress. A **balanced 5-10-10** (low N, higher phosphorus and potassium) strengthens roots and flowers. Apply half the recommended amount at planting, then again in early spring. For native plants like coneflower, skip fertilizer entirely—they thrive in lean soil.

### Gardening Gloves
Bareroot plants and mulch can harbor soil-borne bacteria (e.g., tetanus). Choose **leather gloves with reinforced fingertips** for rock removal; rubber-coated gloves are better for handling moist compost. Avoid cotton—they soak through and cause blisters. Wash gloves after each use to prevent rust on tools.

### Hand Pruners (Bypass Style)
Bypass pruners (blades that slide past each other) make clean cuts without crushing stems, unlike anvil-style pruners. For drought-tolerant plants, deadheading (removing spent flowers) saves energy for root growth. Look for **blades with sap-resistant coating**—lavender and sage produce sticky resins that gum up cheap pruners. Oil the hinge after every use.
